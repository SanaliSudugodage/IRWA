Abstract 36 
Background: Malnutrition and dehydration are widespread in older people, and obesity 37 
is an increasing problem. In clinical practice, it is often unclear which strategies are 38 
suitable and effective in counteracting these key health threats.  39 
Aim: To provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical nutrition and hydration in 40 
older persons in order to prevent and/or treat malnutrition and dehydration. Further, to 41 
address whether weight-reducing interventions are appropriate for overweight or obese 42 
older persons. 43 
Methods: This guideline was developed according to the standard operating procedure 44 
for ESPEN guidelines and consensus papers. A systematic literature search for systematic 45 
reviews and primary studies was performed based on 33 clinical questions in PICO 46 
format. Existing evidence  was graded according to the SIGN grading system. 47 
Recommendations were developed and agreed in a multistage consensus process. 48 
Results: We provide eighty-two evidence-based recommendations for nutritional care in 49 
older persons, covering four main topics: Basic questions and general principles, 50 
recommendations for older persons with malnutrition or at risk of malnutrition, 51 
recommendations for older patients with specific diseases, and recommendations to 52 
prevent, identify and treat dehydration. Overall, we r ecommend that a ll older persons 53 
shall routinely be screened for malnutrition in order to identify an existing risk early. Oral 54 
nutrition can be supported by  nursing interventions, education, nutritional counselling, 55 
food modification and oral nutritional supplements. Enteral nutrition should be initiated 56 
if oral, and parenteral if enteral nutrition is insufficient or impossible and the general 57 
prognosis is altogether favorable.  Dietary restrictions should generally be avoided, and 58 
weight-reducing diets shal l only be considered in obese older persons with weight -59 
related health problems and combined with physical exercise. All older persons should be 60

considered to be at risk of low-intake dehydration and encouraged to consume adequate 61 
amounts of drinks. Genera lly, interventions shall be individualized, comprehensive and 62 
part of a multimodal and multidisciplinary team approach. 63 
Conclusion: A range of effective interventions is available to support adequate nutrition 64 
and hydration in older persons in order to mai ntain or improve nutritional status and 65 
improve clinical course and quality of life. These interventions should be implemented in 66 
clinical practice and routinely used. 67

Introduction 77 
Older persons, usually defined by an age of 65 years or older , are at increased risk of 78 
malnutrition due to many factors. Anorexia of aging is crucial in this context. Particularly 79 
in advanced age and in the case of acute and chronic illness , nutritional problems are 80 
widespread, and a reduced dietary intake in combination with the effects of catabolic 81 
disease rapidly leads to malnutrition (1, 2). Malnutrition is related to poor outcome , e.g. 82 
increased rates of infections, length of hospital stay, duration of convalescence after acute 83 
illness as well as mortality risk (2), and is regarded as one important contributing factor 84 
in the complex etiology of sarcopenia and frailty (1, 3, 4). Reported prevalence rates are 85 
generally below 10% in independently living older persons and increase up to two thirds 86 
in hospitalized older patients (5, 6). 87 
Besides malnutrition, older persons are at increased risk of dehydration for various 88 
reasons, which is also related to serious health consequences (7, 8). Prevalence rates are 89 
also low in independent, community-dwelling older persons but increase to more than 90 
one third in more frail and vulnerable older adults and those in need of care (9). 91 
On the other hand, like in the general population, obesity with its well-known negative 92 
health c onsequences is an increasing problem also in older people, currently affecting 93 
between 18 and 30% of the worldwide population aged 65 years and older (10, 11).  94 
Thus, supporting adequate nutrition including a dequate amounts of food and fluid to 95 
prevent and treat malnutrition and dehydration as well as obesity is an important public 96 
health concern.  97 
The present guideline aims to provide evidence -based recommendations in order to 98 
prevent and/or treat malnutritio n and dehydration in older persons as far as possible . 99

Methodology 103 
The present practical guideline  consists of 82 recommendations and  is based on the 104 
European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism ( ESPEN) guideline on clinical 105 
nutrition and hydration in geriatrics (12). The original guideline was shortened by 106 
focusing the commentaries on the evidence and literature on which the recommendations are 107 
based on. The recommendations were not changed, but the presentation of the content was 108 
transformed into a graphical presentation. The original guideline was developed according 109 
to the standard operating procedure (SOP) for ESPEN guidelines and consensus papers  110 
(13).  111 
A comprehensive, systematic literature search was performed on 4th July 2016 based on 112 
33 clinical question s in PICO (population of interest, interventions, comparisons, 113 
outcomes) format. Existing evidence was graded according to the SIGN (Scottish 114 
Intercollegiate Guidelines Network) grading system. Recommendations were developed 115 
and graded into four classes (A/B/0/GPP). 116 
All recommendations were agreed in a multistage consensus process, which resulted in a 117 
percentage of agreement (%). In brackets, the original recommendation numbers (R1, R2, 118 
…) and the grading is indicated. The guideline process was funded exc lusively by the 119 
ESPEN society. The guideline shortage and dissemination was funded in part by the 120 
United European Gastroenterology ( UEG) society, and also by the ESPEN society. For 121 
further details on methodology, see the full version of the ESPEN guideline  (12) and the 122 
ESPEN SOP (13).  123

1. General principles (Fig. 2) 128 
1.1 Guidance for nutritional intake 129 
1) Guiding value for energy intake in older persons is 30 kcal per kg body weight 130 
and day; this value should be individually adjusted regarding nutritional status, 131 
physical activity level, disease status, and tolerance.  132 
(R1, Grade B, strong consensus 93%) 133 
Commentary 134 
With increasing age, resting energy expenditure (REE) is generally decreasing, mainly due 135 
to decreasing fat -free body mass. In healthy and sick elderly persons, measurements of 136 
REE resulted in about 20 kcal/kg body weight (BW) and day (14-16). Based on usual 137 
physical activity levels between 1.2 and 1.8, total energy expenditure amounts to 24 to 36 138 
kcal/kg. Due to their strong relation to fat-free mass, basal energy requirements are also 139 
influenced by gender and by nutritional status; in fact, REE/kg BW is higher for men than 140 
for women and increases with decreasing body mass index (BMI). For older persons with 141 
underweight (BMI ≤21 kg/m²) energy requirements between 32 and 38 kcal/kg are 142 
assumed (16). In sick older people energy requirements may, on the one hand, be reduced 143 
due to reduced physical activity, and on the other hand be increased due to disease effects 144 
(e.g. inflammation, fever, drug effects). Minimal requirements of ill older persons are 145 
estimated to be between 27 and 30 kcal/kg (16).  146 
Journal Pre-proof
 8 
Based on these figures, about 30 kcal/kg BW is suggested as a rough estimate and general 147 
orientation for energy requirements in older persons. This guiding value needs individual 148 
adjustment regarding all relevant factors . A dequacy of energy intake needs to be 149 
controlled by close monitoring of BW (taking water retention or losses into account), and 150 
intake adapted accordingly.  151 
 152 
2) Protein intake in older persons should be at least 1 g protein per kg BW and day. 153 
The amount should be individually adjusted with regard to nutritional status, 154 
physical activity level, disease status, and tolerance.  155 
(R2, Grade B, strong consensus 100%) 156 
Commentary 157 
Growing evidence from experimental and epidemiological research suggests that older 158 
people might need higher amounts of protein than younger adults for optimal 159 
preservation of lean body mass, body functions, and health. Daily amounts of 1.0 - 1.2 g/kg 160 
BW have been suggested for healthy older persons by several expert groups (17-19). In 161 
case of illness, protein requirements may even be further increased, e.g. due to 162 
inflammation, infections and wounds, however, to which extent is difficult to assess. Very 163 
little is known about the protein needs of frail and ill older persons. Daily amounts of 1.2 164 
- 1.5 g/kg have been suggested for older persons with acute or chronic illness (17, 18) and 165 
up to 2.0 g/kg BW and day in case of severe illness, injury or malnutrition (17).  166 
Until more evidence is available, intake of at least 1.0 g/kg should be ensured in all older 167 
persons, particularly in those at risk of malnutrition, e.g. frail and multimorbid persons, 168 
whose intake is often far below this amount (20-22). Increased requirements, e.g. for 169 
muscle growth with strength training, for tissue regeneration in malnutrition or wound 170 
Journal Pre-proof
 9 
healing or for increased metabolic demands in case of critical illness, should be met by 171 
appropriately increased intake.  172 
It is important to bear in mind that an insufficient intake of energy increases protein 173 
requirements. Thus, regarding protein status , it is important to ensure not only an 174 
adequate intake of protein but also an appropriate energy intake. 175 
 176 
3) For EN, fiber-containing products should be used.  177 
(R3 Grade B, strong consensus 91%) 178 
Commentary  179 
Older patients often suffer from gastrointestinal problems including constipation and 180 
diarrhea. Since dietary fiber may contribute to the normalization of bowel functions, and 181 
intake is usually low in geriatric patients, the importance of an adequate intake of dietary 182 
fiber is emphasized. Daily amounts of 25 g are considered adequate for normal laxation 183 
in adults of ages (23) and can be regarded as guiding value also for older patients.  184 
Also, for enteral nutrition (EN), there is no reason to omit dietary fiber if bowel function 185 
is not compromised. Conversely, fiber -containing products for EN have been shown to 186 
contribute to normal bowel function (24-30) and are, thus, generally reco mmended. In 187 
addition, enterally nourished patients should not be deprived of the well -known 188 
beneficial metabolic effects of dietary fiber.  189 
 190 
4) Provided that there is no specific deficiency, micronutrients should be delivered 191 
according to the recommendation for healthy older persons.  192 
(R4, Grade GPP, strong consensus 91%) 193 
Journal Pre-proof
 10 
Commentary  194 
Dietary recommendations for micronutrients for older persons do not differ from those 195 
for younger adults, however, our knowledge about requirements in very old, frail or ill 196 
persons is poor. Due to an increasing prevalence of gastrointestinal diseases, which are 197 
accompanied by reduced nutrient bioavailability (e.g. atrophic gastritis and impaired 198 
vitamin B12, calcium and iron absorption), older persons are at increased risk of  199 
micronutrient deficiencies, which should be corrected by supplementation. Provided that 200 
there is no specific deficiency, micronutrients should be delivered according to the 201 
recommendation of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) or corresponding 202 
national nutrition societies for healthy older persons (31). 203 
 204 
5) Older women should be offered at least 1.6 L of drinks each day, while older men 205 
should be offered at least 2.0 L of drinks each day unless there is a clinical condition 206 
that requires a different approach.  207 
(R61, Grade B, strong consensus 96% agreement) 208 
Commentary 209 
Daily water intake is required to compensate daily losses by respiration, exudation, urine, 210 
and feces. An individual’s minimum fluid requirement is ‘the amount of water that equals 211 
losses and prevents adverse effects of insufficient water’ (32). We take fluid from drinks 212 
and foods, but drinks or beverages account for 70 to 80% of fluid consumed (33). 213 
The EFSA reviewed the literature and recommended an Adequate Intake (AI) of 2.0 L/day 214 
for women and 2.5 L/day for men of all ages (from a combination of drinking water, 215 
beverages, and food) (32). Assuming 80% of these fluid needs to come from drinks then 216 
women would require 1.6 L/d of drinks, and men 2.0 L/d. Minimal drinks 217 
Journal Pre-proof
 11 
recommendations in women vary from 1.0 L/d in the Nordic countries to 2.2 L/d in the 218 
USA, while in men the range is 1.0 to 3.0 L/d of drinks or beverages (34-38). Given this 219 
variation, the use of the EFSA fluid recommendation seems appropriately cautious in 220 
older adults.  221 
Individual fluid needs are related to energy consumption, water l osses and kidney 222 
function, so larger people may require more fluid. Needs may also be higher in extreme 223 
temperatures (e.g. summer heat) or at times of greater physical activity. Excessive losses 224 
due to, fever, diarrhea, vomiting or severe hemorrhage must a lso be balanced by 225 
additional intake. On the other hand, specific clinical situations, namely heart, and renal 226 
failure may need a restriction of fluid intake. 227

1.2 Basic principles of nutritional care 229 
6) In institutional settings, standard operating procedures for nutritional and 230 
hydration care shall be established and responsibilities well regulated.  231 
(R7, Grade GPP, strong consensus) 232 
Commentary  233 
In order to assure implementation in everyday practice, SOPs should be established. 234 
Nutritional strategie s should be supported by the head of the institution, and 235 
responsibilities well -regulated. Desirably, each geriatric institution should constitute a 236 
multidisciplinary team, including a ll professions involved. Special attention should be 237 
drawn to the interface management, as important information concerning the nutritional 238 
situation is frequently lost in the situation of patients’ transition to another healthcare 239 
sector. 240 
Journal Pre-proof
 12 
In geriatric acute care and rehabilitation hospital units, nutritional assessment and 241 
implementation of a nutritional care plan have been shown to improve energy and protein 242 
intake, serum proteins and health -related quality of life of the patients (39). 243 
Implementation of a screening and treatment protocol at a geriatric hospital unit 244 
including regular team meetings improved BW and hospital -acquired infections 245 
compared to standard care (40). Multidisciplinary nutritional care concepts including 246 
regular team meetings increased dietary intake and improved quality of life in hip fracture 247 
patients (41), and improved nutritional status, wellbeing, and quality of mealtimes in 248 
demented nursing home residents (42). 249

7) Nutritional and hydration care for older persons shall be individualized and 251 
comprehensive in order to ensure adequate nutritional intake, maintain or improve 252 
nutritional status and improve the clinical course and quality of life.  253 
(R8, Grade A, strong consensus 100%) 254 
Commentary 255 
Five RCTs (all performed in the hospital setting) were identified providing evidence for 256 
comprehensive individualized nutritional interventions in older persons with 257 
malnutrition or at risk of malnutrition (43-47). 258 
Three RCTs of low to acceptable quality investigated the effects of comprehensive 259 
individualized nutritional interventions in older hospitalized patients at nutritional risk 260 
with various diagnoses (43, 44) or after acute stroke (45), and reported  positive effects 261 
on energy and protein intake (43, 44) , BW (44, 45) , complications, antibiotic use, 262 
readmissions (44) and functional measures (44, 45) . Additionally, all three studies 263 
showed benefits concerning the quality of life in the group receiving individual nutritional 264 
Journal Pre-proof
 13 
care compared to the group with usual care (43-45). No effect was found regarding the 265 
length of hospital stay (44, 45). In a further RCT of acceptable quality (46), the effect of 266 
additional individual nutritional support by dietetic assistants was investigated in older 267 
hospitalized patients with hip fractures. The study reported increased energy intake and 268 
decreased mortality in the trauma unit and within  four months after discharge in the 269 
intervention group compared to the group with standard care. Bodyweight, grip strength, 270 
complications, and length of hospital stay were however unaffected. Feldblum et al. (47) 271 
extended an individualized nutritional intervention in older internal medical patients to 272 
six months after hospitalization and reported an improved Mini Nutritional Assessment 273 
(MNA) score and reduced mortality in the intervention compared to the control group , 274 
however, no intervention effects on energy or protein intake, BW, and functional 275 
measures.  276

8) Nutritional interventions for older persons should be part of a multimodal and 278 
multidisciplinary team intervention in order to support adequate dietary intake, 279 
maintain or increase BW and improve functional and clinical outcomes.  280 
(R9, Grade B, strong consensus 100%) 281 
Commentary 282 
Nutritional care comprises different approaches (see recommendations 10, 15-17, 22-39, 283 
44), which can complement each other and may require expertise from multiple 284 
professions.  285 
Four relevant RCTs with several sub -studies of low to acceptable quality focus ing on 286 
multimodal and multidisciplinary interventions (combining more than two intervention 287 
strategies) were identified (48-57): a trial combining different components of nutritional 288 
Journal Pre-proof
 14 
care in older patients from hospital admission up to three month s after discharge (63), a 289 
multi-facet intervention consisting of home-made nutritional supplements, oral care and 290 
group exercise in nursing home residents  (49, 50), a multidisciplinary intervention with 291 
nutritional support, physio - and occupational therapy in older malnourished people 292 
receiving home care or living in nursing homes (48, 51) , and a comprehensive 293 
rehabilitation program including nutritional intervention in older patients with hip 294 
fracture. Positive effects on various outcome parameter were reported (e.g. dietary intake 295 
(49, 50, 53) , nutritional status , the incidence of falls  (53, 56), fall-related injuries (56), 296 
health status (55), physical performance (48-51, 57) , social activity (49, 50) , cost-297 
effectiveness (52) and quality of life (48, 51)), results were however not always consistent. 298 
These studies illustrate the complexity of the situation and underline the importance of a 299 
comprehensive treatment approach in older patients. Because of partly inconsistent 300 
results, the evidence grade was reduced from A to B. 301

9) Potential causes of malnutrition and dehydration shall be identified and 303 
eliminated as far as possible.  304 
(R10, Grade GPP, strong consensus 95%) 305 
Commentary  306 
Potential causes of poor intake and/or poor nutritional status in older persons are 307 
manifold and should be explored systematically, e.g. by check -lists and subsequent 308 
assessment and diagnostic clarification. Swallowing evaluation, dental examination, oral 309 
and general health assessment and check -up of medications for potential side effects 310 
impeding adequate nutrition (e.g. by causing anorexia, xerostomia, dysgeusia, 311 
gastrointestinal disorders or somnolence), for example, may uncover eating obstacles and 312 
provide starting points for adequate interventions. In institutionalized older people, 313 
Journal Pre-proof
 15 
eating and feeding problems are widespread and should also be identified, e.g. by informal 314 
observation during meals, and eliminated as far as possible by appropriate remedia l 315 
actions (58).  316 
 317 
10) Dietary restrictions that may limit dietary intake are potentially harmful and 318 
should be avoided.  319 
(R11, Grade GPP, strong consensus 91%) 320 
Commentary  321 
Dietary restrictions are one potent ial cause of malnutrition since they may limit food 322 
choice and eating pleasure and thus bear the risk of limiting dietary intake. As recently 323 
reviewed by Darmon et al.  (59), restrictive diets furthermore seem to be less effective 324 
with increasing age, albeit data about their effects in older persons are rare. In one study, 325 
ambulatory patients older than 75 years following a low salt, low cholesterol or diabetic 326 
diet for 11 ± 6 years were found to be at increased risk of malnutrition compared to age - 327 
and gender -matched controls (60). In a position statement,  the American Dietetic 328 
Association concludes that the liberalization of diet prescriptions for older adults in long-329 
term care may enhance the nutritional status and quality of life (61). Due to the risk of 330 
malnutrition, future studies about the effects of restrictive diets in old age are unlikely, 331 
and itis good clinical practice to liberalize dietary restrictions in older persons in order to 332 
reduce the risk of malnutrition and related loss of fat-free mass and functional decline.  333

11) Health care professionals, as well as informal caregivers, should be offered 335 
nutritional education in order to ensure awareness of and basic knowledge on 336 
nutritional problems and thus promote adequate dietary intake of older persons 337

with malnutrition or at risk of malnutrition.  338 
(R17, Grade B, strong consensus 95%) 339 
Commentary  340 
One of the barriers to proper nutritional support in hospitals is assumed to be a lack of 341 
sufficient education concerning nutrition among all staff groups (62).  342 
Three relevant systematic literature reviews ( SLRs) of high (63, 64) or average quality 343 
(65) were identified, which examined the effectiveness of training for staff in residential 344 
care (64), people with dementia and/or their formal or informal care -givers (63) and 345 
informal carers and community care workers (65). Study designs and results of included 346 
studies were heterogeneous with partly positive effects on dietary intake and nutritional 347 
status. Al together, scientific evidence is presently poor, but education and support for 348 
formal and informal caregivers are rated as one promising strategy among others to 349 
support the adequate dietary intake of older persons with malnutrition or at risk of 350 
malnutrition. For quality assurance reasons, nutritional information and education 351 
should be given by a nutritional expert, e.g. a dietician.  352

2. Prevention and treatment of malnutrition  354 
2.1 Screening and assessment for malnutrition (Fig. 3) 355 
12) All older persons – independent of specific diagnosis and including also 356 
overweight and obese persons – shall routinely be screened for malnutrition with a 357 
validated tool in order to identify those with (risk of) malnutrition.  358 
(R5, Grade GPP, strong consensus 100%) 359 
 360 
The process of nutritional care for older persons consists of several steps which are based 361 
on systematic screening for malnutrition. Independent of specific diagnosis and also in 362 
overweight and obese persons, malnutrition and its risk should  be systematically and 363 
routinely screened at admission to a geriatric institution using a validated tool and 364 
thereafter in regular intervals, depending on the patient´s condition (e.g. every three 365 
months in long -term care residents in stable condition, at least once a year in general 366 
practice) to identify affected individuals early.  367 
 368 
2.2 Assessment, intervention, and monitoring (Fig. 3) 369 
13) A positive malnutrition screening shall be followed by a systematic assessment, 370 
individualized intervention, monitoring and corresponding adjustment of 371 
interventions.  372 
(R6, Grade GPP, strong consensus 100%) 373 
Commentary  374 
Assessment: In individuals who are identified as malnourished or at risk of malnutrition 375 
by screening, a comprehensive nutritional assessment should follow, providing 376 
Journal Pre-proof
 18 
information on kind and severity of malnutrition and its underlying causes as well as on 377 
individual preferences (regarding food and beverages as well as enteral and parenteral 378 
nutrition (PN)) and resources (e.g. chewing and swallowing ability, eating dependence, 379 
gastrointestinal function, severity of disease, general prognosis) for nutritional  therapy. 380 
Dietary intake monitoring (e.g. by plate diagrams) is recommended for several days in 381 
order to estimate the amount of food and fluid consumed (66) and relate dietary intake 382 
to individual requirements (see recommendation 1).  383 
Nutritional intervention: Based on the screening and assessment results, individual goals 384 
regarding dietary intake and BW / BMI should be defined, and an individualized nutrition 385 
care plan developed and implemented in an interdiscipl inary team approach. All aspects 386 
of the patient – physical and mental/psychic, social, clinical as well as ethical – should be 387 
considered, and all options used to ensure an adequate dietary intake. Dietetic, nursing 388 
and medical actions should be implemente d in a coordinated manner (see 389 
recommendation 8). 390 
Monitoring: The intervention process needs to be monitored, and reassessments should 391 
be performed at regular intervals, e.g. after several days, in order to check if goals are 392 
achieved. If this is not the c ase, goals and interventions have to be adjusted according to 393 
experienced problems and the new situation. In the case of EN or PN criteria for 394 
termination of the therapy must be defined (see recommendation 3 4). In the hospital 395 
setting, it is important to initiate adequate nutritional care after discharge at home and to 396 
ensure the continuation of the nutritional strategy started in the hospital (see 397 
recommendation 29). 398 
All interventions should be coordinated and agreed with all parties involved (e.g. medical 399 
specialists, nurses, therapists) (see recommendation 9). Intensive communication with 400 
Journal Pre-proof
 19 
the patient and his or her family should take place during the whole process, in order to 401 
learn and consider the wishes and expectations of the person concerned.  402 
For im plementation in daily routines, these general recommendations have to be 403 
concretized and adapted to the local conditions of each institution. Standard protocols for 404 
nutritional screening, assessment and therapy have to be developed and consistently put 405 
into practice (see recommendation 7).   406

2.3 Prevention and treatment of malnutrition in general (Fig. 4-7) 408 
2.3.1 Supportive interventions (Fig. 4) 409 
14) Older persons with malnutrition or at risk of malnutrition and with eating 410 
dependency in institutions (A) as well as at home (GPP) shall be offered mealtime 411 
assistance in order to support adequate dietary intake.  412 
(R12, Grade A/GPP, strong consensus 100%) 413 
Commentary 414 
Many older persons are restricted in their ability to eat and drink independently due to 415 
functional and cognitive limitations. Support may be needed ranging from adequate 416 
positioning at a table and verbal prompting to direct physical assistance to bring foods 417 
and fluids into the mouth.  418 
Two relevant SLRs of high quality were identified (64, 67, 68) . One (68) examined the 419 
effects of mealtime assistance provided to hospitalized patients (≥65 years) by nurse s, 420 
trained staff or volunteers . Assistance provided at mealtimes included setting up meal 421 
trays, positioning patients in a comfortable position, opening food and beverages, 422 
removing lids, feeding patients, encouraging intake and providing social support at  423 
mealtime. A meta-analysis of four of the five studies included (including one RCT) resulted 424 
Journal Pre-proof
 20 
in significantly improved daily energy and protein intake  in patients with mealtime 425 
assistance. Abbott et al. (64) included six feeding assistance studies. Two RCTs and three 426 
pre-post comparisons described positive effects on dietary intake. Marginal, non -427 
significant improvements in  food intake were also reported from a pre -post trial of 428 
reminiscence therapy during mealtimes in a very small study including seven residents 429 
with dementia.  430 
No intervention studies have been performed among old people in home -care where 431 
malnutrition and risk of malnutrition are also prevalent. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to 432 
assume that eating-dependent older persons living in private households may also benefit 433 
from mealtime assistance.  434

5. Treatment of obesity 1741 
5.1 Indication of weight-reducing diets 1742 
79) In overweight older persons, weight-reducing diets shall be avoided in order to 1743

(R54, Grade GPP, strong consensus 95%) 1745 
Commentary  1746 
Experts generally agree that there is usually no need for overweight older people to lose 1747 
weight (274-278) as meta-analyses indicate that the mortality risk of healthy older people 1748 
is lowest in the overweight range (279-281). Further, weight loss, whether intentional or 1749 
not, enhances the age-related loss of muscle mass and consequently increases the risk of 1750 
sarcopenia, frailty, functional decline, fractures, and malnutrition (276, 282, 283) . 1751 
Moreover, the common weight -regain after a weight -reducing diet is predominantly a 1752 
regain in fat mass and not in lean mass (283). Thus, repeated phases of weight loss and 1753 
regain, called “weight cycling”, might contribute to the development of sarcopenic obesity 1754 
(the presence of reduced muscle mass together with excess fat mass) (283). Therefore, 1755 
and to avoid a progression to obesity, maintaining stable BW is considered desirable for 1756 
overweight older adults (11). A combination of a balanced, nutrient -rich diet providing 1757 
adequate amounts of energy and protein, and physical activity, if possible even exercise, 1758 
is a sound strategy to keep weight stable and to prevent obesity (284).  1759

(R55, Grade GPP, strong consensus 100%) 1764

Current expert recommendations regarding weight reduction in older people primarily 1769
